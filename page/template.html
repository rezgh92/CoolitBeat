Summary

A short-path manifold plate that beats CoolIT's 15 kW result on temperature, pressure drop and capacity

PRISM is an all-copper, single-braze cold plate. It feeds a fine skived fin field through metered lines, so coolant travels 7.7 mm instead of 54 mm. With the same paste CoolIT used, it runs 3.4 K cooler on average and 5.5 K cooler at the hottest point, at about a third of the pressure drop. It costs about 31 % more per plate, so the case is made per kilowatt and at the facility.

The mechanism

Where the win comes from: path length

A split-flow plate sends each parcel of coolant 54 mm along the fins. By the time it reaches the edge it has warmed by the full 12.7 K, and the fins there must be fed by long, narrow channels that cost pressure. PRISM cuts the same field into 14 short cells fed in parallel. Fin geometry is then chosen for heat transfer and fouling, not for pressure drop.

What gets built

Five copper layers, one braze cycle

Process chain

  1. Skive the fin field, plane the tips, gang-cut the 0.3 mm kerfs.
  2. Laser-pierce the grate artwork; this is the only per-package tooling.
  3. Fold the plenum from 0.8 mm copper; draw the arched cap.
  4. Vacuum-braze the stack in one cycle, with a single external seam.
  5. Fly-cut the face to 0.8 mm and cut any HBM pedestal step.
  6. Helium leak, proof, three-point pressure drop and an infrared flow screen on every plate.

One core, three interface tiers

  • Tier 1, drop-in: phase-change pad on a lidded package. 29.0 / 34.8 K.
  • Tier 2, OEM co-design: liquid metal on a nickel-plated face. 23.9 / 28.8 K.
  • Tier 3, chip co-design: stepped pedestal on bare die; removes the lid and one interface.
  • Filtration grades: 0.15 mm channels ship with a 33 µm tray filter; 0.12 and 0.10 mm grades need loops filtered to 33 µm or better.

Honest limits

What PRISM does not win

  • Price per plate: about US$165 against US$126 at 100k a year. Cost per kilowatt is at parity, and the silver-free joint (decided at the first gate) would make it 6–9 % better.
  • A 100 cm² heated area: if CoolIT's heaters were that small, the like-for-like average is a tie. The maximum still edges ahead.
  • Very low flow: below 0.5 LPM/kW the short cells lose the average. The maximum still wins at every flow in the OCP sweep.
  • CoolIT's 4 kW single-die plate: the drop-in core loses there. Only liquid metal or the dedicated small-die variant beats it.
  • Fouling and record: 0.15 mm channels need the supplied 33 µm filter to match a coarser plate's life, and there is no field record yet.
  • 350 W/cm² whole-die flux (2029+): no drop-in grade reaches 85 °C case, though PRISM is about 16 K cooler than a model of split-flow there.

Top risks

  1. Fin-tip braze (voids, wicking, undetected unbonded strips). Gate G1, three backup joint routes, 1.0 mm base fallback at +0.7 K.
  2. CoolIT's patents. The Split-Flow family runs to about Sep 2028 in the US and has a Taiwan member. No US sale before a freedom-to-operate opinion at M4.
  3. Micro-channel lids at NVIDIA could replace lidded plates from 2027. A US$0.4–0.6 M lid-insert study hedges it.
  4. Benchmark ambiguity. CoolIT's area and interface are unpublished. We test two vehicle sizes and buy incumbent plates to measure ourselves.

Budget and odds

  • US$2.3 M to the first gate; US$8.4–10.7 M for 12 months with contingency; US$11.4–14.5 M to a qualified product at M18.
  • About 18 people: thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, test, reliability, quality, IP.
  • Probability of the decisive PCM result at M12: about 0.43. Of beating CoolIT on PCM average, maximum and pressure drop: about 0.68.
  • The likeliest failure is a 1–3 month slip at the braze gate, not a thermal miss.
